## Overview

Quality metrics provide **quantitative** measures of algorithm performance beyond raw fitness values. They assess both **convergence** (proximity to optimal front) and **diversity** (spread of solutions).

## 1. `hypervolume_trend.pdf`

### Description
Tracks the **hypervolume indicator** over generations - the gold standard metric for multi-objective optimization.

### Axes
- **X-axis**: Generation number (0 = initial population)
- **Y-axis**: Hypervolume Indicator (area/volume units)
  - **Higher is better** (more objective space dominated)
  
### Visual Elements
- **Blue line with markers**: Hypervolume evolution
- **Light blue fill**: Area under curve
- **Yellow box with arrow**: Best (maximum) hypervolume achieved
- **White info box**: Initial, final, and improvement percentage

### Mathematical Definition

The hypervolume (also called **S-metric** or **Lebesgue measure**) is the volume of objective space dominated by the Pareto front relative to a reference point:

$$
HV(\mathcal{P}, \vec{r}) = \lambda\left(\bigcup_{x \in \mathcal{P}} \{y \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid x \preceq y \preceq \vec{r}\}\right)
$$

where:
- $\mathcal{P}$ = Pareto front (set of non-dominated solutions)
- $\vec{r}$ = reference point (worst-case scenario)
- $\lambda$ = Lebesgue measure (area in 2D, volume in 3D, etc.)
- $\preceq$ = dominance relation

**For 2D problems** (like course scheduling):
$$
HV = \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{P}|} (f_1^i - f_1^{i-1})(r_2 - f_2^i)
$$
where solutions are sorted by $f_1$ in ascending order.

### Computation Details
- **Reference point** $\vec{r}$: Typically set to $(r_1, r_2)$ where:
  - $r_1$ = max hard violations in population + margin
  - $r_2$ = max soft penalty in population + margin
  
- **Algorithm**: Uses rectangular integration (2D case)

### How to Interpret

#### Trend Patterns
1. **Steep increase → plateau**: Good convergence, reached stable front
2. **Continuous increase**: Still improving, may need more generations
3. **Flat line**: Stagnation, algorithm stuck
4. **Fluctuations**: Population instability, poor diversity preservation

#### Absolute Values
- **High HV** (relative to reference point): Good convergence + diversity
- **Low HV**: Poor performance or difficult problem
- **Comparison**: Only meaningful with same reference point

#### Improvement Metric
The info box shows:
```
Initial: 1250.45
Final: 3847.22
Improvement: 207.5%
```
- **> 100%**: Significant improvement (good)
- **50-100%**: Moderate improvement (acceptable)
- **< 50%**: Weak improvement (needs tuning)

### Why Hypervolume is the Gold Standard
1. **Combines convergence + diversity** in single metric
2. **Pareto-compliant**: Better front → higher HV (provably)
3. **Sensitive to improvements** anywhere on front
4. **Unbiased**: Doesn't favor specific regions of objective space

### Related CSV Data
- **File**: `data/metrics.csv`
- **Column**: `hypervolume`
- **Format**: One value per generation

## 2. `spacing_trend.pdf`

### Description
Measures the **uniformity** of Pareto front distribution. Lower values indicate more evenly spaced solutions.

### Axes
- **X-axis**: Generation number
- **Y-axis**: Spacing Metric (distance units)
  - **Lower is better** (more uniform distribution)

### Visual Elements
- **Green line with square markers**: Spacing evolution
- **Light green fill**: Area emphasis
- **Yellow box with arrow**: Best (minimum) spacing
- **White info box**: Initial, final, and improvement percentage

### Mathematical Definition

Spacing measures the standard deviation of nearest-neighbor distances:

$$
S = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|-1} \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{P}|} (d_i - \bar{d})^2}
$$

where:
- $d_i$ = distance from solution $i$ to its nearest neighbor
- $\bar{d}$ = mean of all $d_i$
- $|\mathcal{P}|$ = number of Pareto solutions

**Nearest-neighbor distance**:
$$
d_i = \min_{j \neq i} \|\vec{f}(x_i) - \vec{f}(x_j)\|_2
$$

Using Euclidean norm:
$$
\|f(x_i) - f(x_j)\|_2 = \sqrt{(f_1^i - f_1^j)^2 + (f_2^i - f_2^j)^2}
$$

### How to Interpret

#### Ideal Spacing
- **S = 0**: Perfect uniform distribution (rare in practice)
- **S < 1.0**: Very good uniformity (typical goal)
- **S > 5.0**: Poor uniformity, gaps in front

#### Trend Analysis
1. **Decreasing trend**: Algorithm improving distribution
2. **Flat at low value**: Stable, uniform distribution achieved
3. **Increasing trend**: Losing diversity (warning sign)
4. **Erratic**: Population instability

#### Practical Implications
- **Low spacing**: Decision-maker has smooth trade-off curve
- **High spacing**: Large gaps, missing intermediate solutions
- **Example**: Spacing of 0.5 means solutions differ by ~0.5 units on average

### Why Spacing Matters
In practical scheduling:
- **Low spacing**: Can find schedule matching any priority balance
- **High spacing**: Forced to choose between extreme options
- **Example**: Gap between (0 hard, 200 soft) and (5 hard, 100 soft) means no options with 1-4 hard violations

## 3. `spacing_distribution.pdf`

### Description
Histogram of nearest-neighbor distances in the **final** Pareto front.

### Axes
- **X-axis**: Nearest-Neighbor Distance (distance units)
- **Y-axis**: Frequency (count of solutions)

### Visual Elements
- **Green bars**: Histogram of distances
- **Red dashed line**: Mean distance
- **White info box**: Spacing metric, mean, standard deviation

### How to Interpret

#### Distribution Shape
- **Narrow peak**: Uniform spacing (ideal)
- **Wide spread**: Variable spacing (some gaps)
- **Multiple peaks**: Clustered solutions with gaps between
- **Long tail**: Few solutions far from others

#### Statistics
```
Spacing: 0.342
Mean: 1.25
Std: 0.45
```
- **Low Std**: Consistent spacing
- **High Std**: Irregular spacing
- **Spacing ≈ Std**: Expected relationship (spacing is std of these distances)

#### Example Interpretations
1. **All bars near mean**: Excellent uniformity
2. **Few bars at 0, most at higher values**: Some duplicate solutions
3. **Bimodal distribution**: Two clusters of solutions

## 5. `hypervolume_trend.pdf` (Multi-Run Variant)

### Description
When comparing multiple runs, shows mean hypervolume with confidence intervals.

### Visual Elements
- **Light colored lines**: Individual runs
- **Bold blue line**: Mean across runs
- **Blue band**: 95% confidence interval
- **Info box**: Final mean, std, number of runs

### Mathematical Background

**Confidence Interval** (95%):
$$
CI = \bar{HV} \pm 1.96 \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

where:
- $\bar{HV}$ = mean hypervolume across runs
- $\sigma$ = standard deviation
- $n$ = number of runs
- $1.96$ = z-score for 95% confidence

### How to Interpret
- **Narrow band**: Consistent performance across runs
- **Wide band**: High variance, algorithm unstable
- **Individual lines mostly in band**: Normal variation
- **Individual lines outside band**: Outlier runs

## 7. `feasibility_evolution.pdf`

### Description
Tracks the percentage of **feasible solutions** (zero hard violations) in the population over generations.

### Axes
- **X-axis**: Generation number
- **Y-axis**: Feasibility Rate (0-100%)
  - **Higher is better** (more feasible solutions)

### Visual Elements
- **Cyan line with circular markers**: Feasibility rate
- **Light cyan fill**: Area under curve
- **Green dashed line at 100%**: Perfect feasibility target
- **White info box**: Final, max, and average feasibility rates

### Mathematical Definition

$$
\text{Feasibility Rate}(\text{gen}) = \frac{|\{x \in P_{\text{gen}} \mid f_1(x) = 0\}|}{|P_{\text{gen}}|} \times 100\%
$$

where:
- $P_{\text{gen}}$ = population at generation
- $f_1(x)$ = hard constraint violations

### How to Interpret

#### Target Values
- **100%**: All solutions feasible (ideal, rare)
- **> 80%**: Excellent feasibility
- **50-80%**: Good feasibility
- **< 50%**: Poor feasibility, problem may be over-constrained

#### Trend Patterns
1. **Rising to plateau**: Successfully finding feasible region
2. **Fluctuating**: Unstable, repair operators struggling
3. **Starting high, staying high**: Easy problem, good initial population
4. **Starting low, ending high**: Repair operators working well
5. **Always low**: Problem infeasible or repair operators insufficient

#### Relationship to Pareto Front
- **High feasibility rate**: Pareto front likely has $f_1 = 0$ solutions
- **Low feasibility rate**: Pareto front may have $f_1 > 0$ (infeasible problem)

### Practical Implications
- **Course scheduling**: 100% feasibility rarely possible with real constraints
- **Typical good result**: 60-90% feasibility rate
- **If < 30%**: Check input data for impossible requirements

## Comparative Summary

| Metric | What It Measures | Best Value | Trend Goal |
|--------|------------------|------------|------------|
| **Hypervolume** | Overall quality (convergence + diversity) | Higher | Increasing |
| **Spacing** | Uniformity of solutions | Lower | Decreasing |
| **Feasibility Rate** | Percentage of feasible solutions | 100% | Increasing |

### Complementary Nature
- **High HV + Low Spacing**: Ideal - good front, well distributed
- **High HV + High Spacing**: Good convergence but gaps
- **Low HV + Low Spacing**: Poor front but uniform
- **Low HV + High Spacing**: Poor in all aspects

---

## Advanced: Metric Relationships

### Hypervolume vs. Spacing
- **Independent metrics**: Can have high HV with poor spacing
- **HV prioritizes convergence**: Prefers solutions closer to ideal
- **Spacing ignores quality**: Only cares about distribution

### Formal Properties

#### Hypervolume Compliance
**Theorem**: If Pareto front A strictly dominates front B, then $HV(A) > HV(B)$

**Weak monotonicity**: Adding non-dominated solution cannot decrease HV

#### Spacing Properties
- **Non-monotonic**: Adding solution can increase spacing
- **Scale-dependent**: Units matter for interpretation
- **Sensitive to outliers**: One far solution affects metric
